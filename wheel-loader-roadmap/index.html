Chapter 1

Why this document exists.

Feature requests reach us as phone calls, site visits and half sentences in meetings. They get lost, or they get repeated until the loudest one wins. This is the register, with a source against every line and a verdict against every line.

What it is

Every request in here came from a named source: a customer, an operator, Volvo, or our own pipeline. Where the request is a quote, the quote is given. Where the source is a meeting, the meeting and the date are given. Nothing in here is invented to fill a gap.

The companion document, Wheel loader operations, describes how a site runs and what the customer measures. Read that first if you are new. This document assumes it.

The three buckets

Every request gets exactly one bucket. A request without a bucket has not been discussed.

Bucket AShip nowAlready possible with what we have, low effort, customer value this quarter. If it needs a new sensor or a new model, it is not in here.
Bucket BBuild nextHas a named customer and money behind it. Goes on the roadmap with an owner and a date.
Bucket CPark, and say whyInteresting, but no customer today, or blocked by something outside our control. Parked with a written reason so it does not come back as a surprise.

How to read a verdict

Each request carries a verdict in a red-edged box. The verdict is the honest position, not the optimistic one. Where the outside world has already solved something, the verdict says so and names who. Where something is an unsolved research problem, the verdict says that too, because committing engineering time to a thirty-year problem on a customer timeline is how a roadmap dies.

What this document is not

It is not a commitment. Dates belong in the product board, not here. It is not a customer document either. It names customers, quotes operators and states where we fall short, so it stays inside Hive.

Chapter 3 · Bucket A

Ship now.

Five things we can do with the machines and the stack we already have. None of them needs a new sensor. Together they close three customer complaints and give every commercial conversation a number it does not have today.

R01 · Load-complete signal to the truck driver
Load-out sites · asked repeatedly, never specified
Ship now

When the bucket has filled a truck, the driver has to know. A human operator honks. We have no defined behaviour, so every remote operator improvises.

There is no industry standard to match. Caterpillar and Komatsu both solved the surrounding problem with in-cab screens rather than horn codes, so there is no protocol we are failing to implement.

Verdict. The horn already works. Make it a documented, deliberate behaviour in the remote operator interface with a defined pattern, and write it into the operator procedure. This is a day of work and it removes a question we get on every site visit.
R02 · One push-to-talk solution across all operations
Every site · improvised per site today
Ship now

The remote operator has to reach the site: the weighbridge, the site manager, a truck driver at the stockpile. Today this is a phone or whatever radio the site already runs.

Zello is the pragmatic choice. Software only, works over Wi-Fi and cellular, app and API based, no proprietary radio hardware, and the largest installed base. Motorola WAVE PTX only matters where a quarry already runs Motorola radios and we have to bridge to them.

Verdict. Pick one, integrate it once centrally, deploy it everywhere. No purpose-built integration exists between any push-to-talk platform and a remote heavy-equipment console, so this is a small build whichever way we go. Doing it per site is the expensive version.
R03 · Idle shutdown and fuel per tonne reporting
Commercial · every deal, no number today
Ship now

An idling loader burns roughly a quarter to two fifths of its working fuel rate while producing nothing. Unmanaged fleets sit idle anywhere from ten to sixty per cent of operating hours depending on which source you believe. A remote operator does not leave a machine idling between assignments the way a bored cab operator might, so this is a saving we should already be delivering and cannot currently prove.

Fuel per tonne, not fuel per hour, is the metric the trade actually uses, because it ties fuel to output. Volvo CareTrack already exposes engine hours, fuel and idle ratio, reachable through the AEMP 2.0 and ISO 15143-3 telematics interface that fleet managers already plug into.

Verdict. Build our own before-and-after measurement at Hadeland and quote our own number. The published percentages are vendor-derived and inconsistent, so they are directionally useful and not quotable. Separately: nobody has published anything credible on engine wear from frequent automatic start-stop on heavy off-highway diesels, so do not promise automatic shutdown until an engineer has an opinion on it.
R04 · Remove the Hive-side idle stop that kills a cold machine
Feiring Bruk · operator · 17 Aug 2026
Ship now

The machine dies after one to five minutes of idling from a cold start. The operator's workaround is to hold the throttle. It matters most when the engine is cold and needs to warm up, which is exactly when the operator cannot do anything else.

Volvo's own auto-stop is set to 49 minutes, so this timer is ours, not theirs.

Verdict. Ours to fix, and small. It is also the thing the operator mentions first, which makes it worth more than its size.
R05 · Stop the false emergency-stop alarms
Feiring Bruk · operator · 17 Aug 2026
Ship now
Vi får varsler om at vi har trykket på røde og svarte knapper, både i hosten og i maskinen, men det har vi jo da beviselig ikke gjort.

The root cause was identified in the same meeting: the safety card at PLC level goes to safe state, all inputs and outputs go high, and we publish that as a button press.

Verdict. Root cause is known and the fix is in our software. Every false alarm costs operator trust, and operator trust is the thing that decides whether a site defends us internally.

The four that block signed money

R06 · Bucket weight, to invoice off the machine
Feiring Bruk · 17 and 27 Aug 2026
Build next

This is the hardest commercial blocker we have. Until weight is integrated we are not allowed to load paying customers at all. Feiring's own goal is bigger than a readout: they want to remove their weighbridges and invoice straight off the machine.

For at vi skal få det til i pilotprosjektet, så må vi på et eller annet vis få en indikasjon på hvor mye last vi laster opp i lastbilen. For at ikke han skal måtte kjøre ned til vekta, og så komme tilbake igjen og måtte tømme av.
[Eivind Madsen, 17 Aug 2026]

Volvo has already said no to giving us the signals. We asked for access and were refused. The alternative Volvo offered is their own tablet retrofit at roughly 50,000. [Eivind Madsen, 17 Aug 2026]

The wider picture matters here. Volvo launched Certifiable On-Board Weighing in 2026, type-approved by a notified body under OIML R51 and aligned with the EU Measuring Instruments Directive, covering L110 to L260 in Europe. Certified is the operative word, because invoicing off a machine is legal-for-trade territory and only certified equipment can do it. Rollout is market by market, gated on local metrology certification. Trimble LOADRITE and VEI have had legal-for-trade options for years.

Verdict. Three routes, not one: buy Volvo's tablet, retrofit a third-party certified scale, or build our own. Pick one deliberately rather than drifting. Two cautions. Quoted accuracy of about one per cent is a lab figure and will not survive wet or frozen material and uneven bucket fill, and we found no independent non-vendor audit. And we must not claim invoicing-grade tonnage on any machine whose scale is not certified in that country.
R07 · Brakes that work from the remote station
Feiring Bruk · operator Kenneth · 17 Aug 2026
Build next

Three pedals, two of them brakes. One is too hard, the other behaves unstably, and there is no feel through either.

This is one of three things the Feiring pilot hangs on. When the platform status was reviewed on 27 August, no date was given for it.

Verdict. Feel is the whole problem. A remote operator cannot modulate a pedal they cannot feel, so this is an interface design task as much as a control task. It needs a date, and it has not had one since August.
R08 · Network loss must actually stop the machine
Feiring Bruk · safety finding · 17 Aug 2026
Build next
Vi får Network Loss, men maskinen stopper ikke. Litt ugreit. Det skal den gjøre.

Open underneath it: nobody has confirmed the network is good enough. The coverage measurements that exist were taken with a mobile phone, not with a professional survey. The question of whether the loss is 5G or something else is unanswered.

Verdict. Safety-critical and not negotiable. It also has a second half: get a real coverage survey done before the next site goes live, because we are currently guessing about the link our safe state depends on. Note too that the 2027 regulation puts the connectivity layer itself inside the safety case, so this is not infrastructure sitting outside the argument.
R09 · Emergency stop must propagate to attached equipment
Presis Veidrift · Øveråsen snow blower · 17 Aug 2026
Build next

Today, an emergency stop on the machine does not stop the snow blower mounted on it. The attachment keeps running.

Verdict. Safety-critical, and it generalises. Every attachment we ever run raises the same question, so solve it as an interface rule for attachments rather than as one fix for one blower.

What the operator cannot see

R10 · Distance to the truck body, and a camera that sees into it
Feiring Bruk · operator Kenneth · 17 Aug 2026
Build next
Siden jeg skal kjøre nærme nord med skuffa, jeg vet ikke om det er 1 meter fra eller 3 meter fra.
Første gangen vi prøvde å laste, det var jo som å laste i blinde, nesten.

A new camera position on the lift arm is being tested on an L110, on the reasoning that from there you look straight down into the truck body.

Worth knowing what already exists. Caterpillar's Truck Spotting places a GPS spot point under the bucket and shows the truck driver where to reverse to on an in-cab display with a camera overlay. Komatsu's FrontRunner has the loader operator spot the incoming autonomous truck from a touchscreen and release it when loaded. Both have been productised for years.

Verdict. Build it as table stakes, not as a differentiator, and do not plan on integrating with either of those systems: they are closed ecosystems and will not expose spotting interfaces to a third-party system on a competitor's loader. Our wedge is supervised remote operation of Volvo loaders. Truck coordination is the price of entry.
R11 · Cameras that work in dark, rain, dust and low sun
Feiring Bruk 17 Aug · Volvo asked the same question 19 Aug
Build next
Vi må se på hele problematikken rundt dette med kamera og vær og lys og mørke og sollys.
[Eivind Madsen]

A specific finding, identified months before it was raised: a person may not be visible on camera in bright daylight. Volvo asked us directly how we handle dust and night shifts; our answer was that we use the same cameras with no dedicated sensors, because the vision encoder is pre-trained on ordinary images.

Order this correctly. Dust is the number one perception risk in a quarry, ahead of rain and snow. It is well documented that dust coats the LiDAR surface and forms a mist that puts noise into the point cloud and degrades detection materially. The accepted mitigation is sensor fusion rather than any single sensor: radar penetrates rain, fog and dust best, camera degrades hardest in low light, glare and dust, LiDAR sits between them. Red-green-blue and LiDAR fusion for dust de-filtering has been demonstrated specifically in autonomous excavation, so it is directly relevant rather than borrowed from automotive.

Verdict. Two gaps are ours to close and nobody else's. Nobody has published perception failure rates at wheel-loader scale, only at open-pit haul-truck scale. And Nordic rain and snow, which is our actual home market, is under-published next to hot dusty mining. We cannot read our way out of either. Until we have our own validation, we make no claim to a customer that we have solved dust.
R19 · Depth perception for the remote operator
Operators, all sites
Build next

Stereo camera plus low latency is the proven baseline. The literature is explicit that a single monocular feed is not enough, because of narrow field of view and no stereoscopic depth cue, which is precisely the complaint in R10.

Latency is repeatedly named as the dominant driver of teleoperation quality problems, causing operator discomfort and in extreme cases damage to the machine. No published universal latency threshold exists, because it is task and machine specific.

Verdict. Measure and publish our own round-trip number, camera to decision, and use it as a competitive and safety talking point. Do not claim an external industry standard threshold, because there is not one. Gaussian splatting and neural radiance fields for site reconstruction are real research directions for operator situational awareness, but there is no evidence we need them to be competitive. Two to three year horizon, not a current gap.
R13 · Tilt and gyro indicator in the remote station
Presis Veidrift · 17 Aug 2026
Build next
Operatøren må vite om maskinen står sånn eller sånn når han begynner å løfte den der 6-toms snøfreseren fram. Eller om han står på siden og du begynner å løfte, og så tipper han.

The Volvo A30 has this built in and Volvo sells it as an option, so the need is established outside us.

Verdict. Small, and it is a tipping risk, which puts it above its size. It also connects to a larger question worth answering once: does our pose estimation fuse anything, or does it rely on satellite positioning alone? Satellite positioning degrades badly against a quarry face, which is exactly where we work. If we are relying on it alone, that is a quiet risk on every site.

R17 · Optimal load replaces bucket fill as the KPI
Stage model work · 3 Sep 2026
Build next
Viktig er på en måte at ikke vi laster dumperen, eller laster trucken for mye og ikke for lite. Jeg tror det er viktigere enn bucket fill, men optimal loading.

The proposed wording is optimal load of all trucks. It sits inside a four-stage rollout model whose KPIs are teleoperation uptime, cycle time and optimal load.

Verdict. Right metric. A full bucket that overloads a truck is a worse outcome than a slightly lighter one, and bucket fill cannot see the difference. It also depends on R06, because optimal load without weight is an opinion.
R18 · Load distribution inside the truck body
Load-out sites
Build next

Where the material sits in the body, not just how much of it there is. Haul bodies are engineered to be self-centring, and a standard three-drop pattern of front, back then centre is taught to human operators to avoid tyre-load imbalance and stress on the body when it lifts.

Verdict. There is an existing human procedure to encode, which makes this far cheaper than it sounds. Start by teaching the remote operator the three-drop pattern and only then consider automating it.
R20 · Cycle time that matches manual operation
Feiring Bruk · milestone 2, Lørenskog
Build next

Milestone 2 is the main plant at Lørenskog, where 600 trucks leave the site every day, and the requirement is to match the cycle time they achieve there.

Volvo's own measured view is worth carrying into this. On a single cycle, teleoperation has never been seen on par, perhaps 85 per cent at best. But compared at shift level there is typically 30 to 50 per cent downtime to work with, which is where the comparison can be won.

Verdict. Argue at shift level and say so plainly, rather than letting a customer benchmark us cycle against cycle. That is not a trick, it is the correct unit of comparison for a machine that does not take breaks. R14 has to be able to report both.

Chapter 5 · Bucket C

Park, and say why.

Five things that keep coming back. Each is parked with a written reason, so the next person who raises it gets an answer instead of a shrug.

R26 · Pile analysis and an optimal loading plan
Product · recurring
Park as autonomy, keep as assistance

The idea: analyse the face or the pile in front of the machine, work out the best way to attack it, and programme the resulting pattern into the locomotion stack so the machine runs a full cycle itself.

The fact that should govern this. Autonomous bucket filling in granular material is described in the research literature, including work done with Volvo, as an open problem of roughly three decades. Nobody has solved it, including organisations with far more resource than us. Volvo's own framing of why is useful: in virgin material you do not know what is in there, and half-buried objects are identified by a human who recognises them.

Verdict. This is good news for positioning and bad news for scheduling. Our supervised model keeps a human making the judgement call, so we can say honestly that pure autonomous scooping is unsolved while still delivering the outcome. What is achievable is assistance: analyse the pile, propose an attack point and a plan, let the operator accept it. Keep it advisory and outside the control loop. That is also what keeps it out of the notified-body category in chapter 9, because advisory machine learning is not machine learning ensuring a safety function.
R27 · Starlink on every machine
Product · recurring
Not engineering work

One thing here is urgent and it is not a feature. Starlink's terms of service prohibit in-motion use except on the hardware SpaceX has approved for it, currently the Flat High Performance terminal on a mobility or vehicle plan. Standard and recreational kits are not approved in motion, and short repositioning inside a quarry plausibly counts as in motion.

Beyond that, Starlink quotes latency in the region of 20 to 26 milliseconds and markets itself directly at heavy plant. But nobody has published latency or reliability figures for a moving loader inside a pit, with terrain blockage, vibration and a lift arm swinging over the antenna.

Verdict. There is no integration to build with SpaceX. This is a procurement decision, a plan, and a mount. Do not let it be scoped as engineering. The action this week is to confirm which terminal and mount are actually fitted to our machines, because if it is not the in-motion hardware we have a live terms-of-service risk.
R28 · Telling truck drivers where to stand
Load-out sites
Folds into R10

Same family as truck spotting. The cheap first version is the remote operator placing a marker the driver can see, not a coordination system.

Verdict. Do not scope it separately. It is the second half of R10 and it should ship with it.
R29 · Patting the load down so it does not blow off
Product · recurring
Ask one customer first

Tapping the top of a loaded body with the bucket so fine material does not blow off on the road. We found no source describing this as a productised feature anywhere, which means it is either genuinely novel or genuinely marginal.

Verdict. Settle it by asking one site manager whether they lose material on the road today, not by estimating it. That is a five-minute question on the next site visit and it decides whether this is a small nice-to-have or nothing.
R30 · Ramp and haul-road building
Product · recurring
Assisted, never autonomous

Ramp and haul-road maintenance inside an active quarry appears to remain a human-judgement task, and we found no evidence of anyone automating it. The reason is structural: there is no defined target geometry and compaction is a judgement call, unlike a civil engineering project where grade control tools such as Trimble Earthworks or Topcon have a design surface to work to.

Verdict. Grade-control-assisted human operation is the realistic version, and that is fine for our positioning as long as nobody sells it as autonomous. Park the autonomous version and say why every time it returns.

Chapter 7

What Volvo said.

Two days with Volvo CE in August and an interface session in late August. They gave us a timetable, a refusal, and one technical objection that is worth more than the rest of it combined.

What they can deliver, and when

Three workstreams: interface, legal, and CE marking. Legal is parked until the proof-of-value agreement is in place. The CAN specification exists and can be shared now. The electrical interface specification is roughly two weeks of work if they are given a deadline. The operations-centre ECU is targeted for November with a prototype in October, and it exists primarily for legal reasons rather than technical ones. Steer-by-wire exists on an excavator today; on a wheel loader it arrives in 2028.

What they will not do

They refused access to the weight signals. The alternative offered is their own tablet retrofit at roughly 50,000. This is the single most consequential sentence in the Volvo material, because it decides the shape of R06.

They want a brand-agnostic partner, not a Volvo-only one. Their reasoning is a customer with four Volvo loaders and one Caterpillar who wants all five autonomous. They are also explicit that automation is not their core business today and that they are on the learning curve themselves, and that scaling has to run through the dealer network rather than a central team, which means the dealer has to make money on it or nothing happens.

The objection worth answering

Their sharpest technical criticism is that training on teleoperation data is our biggest weakness. Asked how he knew, the answer was: I measured it. He then offered to deliver the same data flow captured directly on the machine with an operator in the cab, arguing the model would not be worse for it.

Open, and the most valuable unclosed item in this document. That offer has not been taken up or refused. It is either a significant gift or a significant problem with our data strategy, and either way we should find out which.

Their other observations

On cycle time: never seen on par, perhaps 85 per cent, but comparing at shift level gives 30 to 50 per cent downtime to work with. On virgin material: you do not know what is in there, and a human recognises a half-buried object for what it is. On dust and night shifts, they asked us directly what we do, and our answer was that we use the same cameras. They want production follow-up split per flow. They asked whether we can position a machine indoors, because roofing requirements are increasing in recycling and mining. And they asked what an hour of labour has to cost for this to scale, which we could not answer either.

What Volvo did not say. Nothing about work lighting, nothing about LiDAR placement, bracket design or a sensor bill of materials. Everything in this document about light and darkness comes from our own side. If we want Volvo's position on what their machines can host, power and give bus access to, we have to ask them directly. It is not in the public record and it is not in these meetings.

Chapter 9

Safety, and the 2027 regulation.

One part of this is a 2027 deadline. The other part applies today, on every machine we have already modified, and that is the part nobody has documented.

Where we stand today

We deliver performance level c today, in a closed area. Emergency stop, the safety controller and the new board are at performance level d and the corresponding integrity level. The goal is a wider confined-area classification in the first half of next year, with a hybrid handover where traffic is mixed.

The deadline

The EU Machinery Regulation 2023/1230 entered into force in July 2023 and becomes directly applicable across all member states on 20 January 2027, replacing the Machinery Directive. There is no grace period for machines placed on the market after that date, and because it is a Regulation rather than a Directive it binds directly with no national variation. Its scope explicitly covers autonomous mobile machinery, remote-controlled machinery, cybersecurity of safety functions, and self-evolving behaviour.

The question that decides the cost

Machinery whose safety components have fully or partially self-evolving machine-learning behaviour ensuring safety functions falls into a high-risk annex, and requires mandatory notified-body assessment. Self-declaration is not enough.

Whether we land inside that category depends entirely on whether our machine learning touches safety-critical decisions, or only advisory ones such as suggesting a path, with deterministic emergency stop and geofencing underneath. The difference between self-declaration against a technical file and a notified-body type examination is very large in both cost and elapsed time. Public sources do not settle it.

Open. This needs a formal legal or notified-body opinion on our specific architecture. It is not something to decide internally, and it shapes how we are allowed to build R26.

The part that applies now

Substantial modification is an existing principle, not a 2027 one. Whoever modifies a machine so that its function or its risk level changes becomes a partial manufacturer for the modified part: fresh risk assessment, their own declaration of conformity, their own marking for that scope. The original manufacturer's marking remains valid for the unmodified base machine.

The practical reading is that we are very likely the manufacturer of record for the kit we install, on every retrofit, today. We could find no public case of a comparable retrofit autonomy company publishing how they handle this in Europe, because most of them are United States first, where the Regulation does not apply. So there is little precedent to copy.

The favourable half

The Regulation requires a supervisory function for autonomous mobile machinery: a human able to monitor activity in real time and intervene to stop, reposition or restart. That is close to a description of what we already do. If the annex question resolves favourably, built for the human-supervisory model the regulation requires becomes a sales asset rather than a risk.

Article and annex numbering in this chapter is paraphrased from secondary sources. Check the Official Journal text before quoting a number to a customer or a regulator.

Open safety items from the sites

Network loss does not stop the machine (R08). Emergency stop does not propagate to attached equipment (R09). False emergency-stop alarms from the safety controller going to safe state (R05). A person may not be detected on camera in bright daylight (R11). No professional coverage survey has been done on any site. Labelling, manuals and a declaration of conformity are outstanding for Presis (R25).

Chapter 10

Open questions.

Seven questions that change what we build. None of them needs research to answer, only a person who knows.

  1. Which of the five Bucket A items can ship inside 30 days? If the answer is none, the bucket is wrong.
  2. Does our pose estimation fuse anything, or is it satellite positioning alone? A quarry face blocks the sky. If we are relying on satellites alone, that is a quiet risk on every site we operate.
  3. Which Starlink terminal and mount are fitted to our machines right now? Anything other than the in-motion approved hardware is a live terms-of-service risk.
  4. What is our measured round-trip teleoperation latency, as a number? We should be able to say it without hedging.
  5. Which of the three weighing routes do we take? Volvo's tablet, a certified third-party retrofit, or our own build. Volvo has already refused signal access, so that route is closed.
  6. Who owns pile analysis, and is it explicitly advisory rather than in the control loop? The answer decides whether chapter 9 gets more expensive.
  7. What do we log today against the one-year and five-year retention requirements? If the answer is nothing, that is a build, not a policy.
And one for Volvo. Do we accept the offer to capture training data directly on the machine with an operator in the cab? It has been open since 19 August.
