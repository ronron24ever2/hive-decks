Chapter 1

Why this document exists.

Engineering is about to set the wheel loader roadmap and build the infrastructure it runs on. Sales talks to the customer every day. This is the handover between the two, kept alive.

The problem we are paid to solve

A quarry, a recycling plant or an asphalt plant is a production unit. Material has to move every hour the plant runs. The wheel loader keeps the crusher fed, the stockpiles in order and the trucks loaded. When it stops, the plant stops.

Today one person sits in that cab all shift. The site manager calls that person and says what to do for the next two to four hours. There is no system in between. [Ronny to Mykhaylo, 11 Sep 2026]

Who it is for

The site manager or production lead who owns the tonnage number. Behind them: the plant owner who signs, HSE who sets the rules around the machine, and the operators who work next to it.

What the customer does today, without us

Hires and rosters operators. Assigns work by phone, radio or WhatsApp. Tracks tonnes through the weighbridge and the crusher. Accepts that a machine with no operator is a machine that does nothing.

What Hive does, in one sentence

We take charge of the machine in operation. A retrofitted Volvo wheel loader is run from our operations centre in Kristiansand under supervised autonomy, with a person in the loop who takes the site manager's call exactly as the cab operator did. The customer keeps their system. [terminology memo, 2026]

Why now

One machine is in validation at Feiring Bruk, one has run 18 months at Yara. Behind the next signatures sit fleets: NCC with about 300 own wheel loaders, SigmaRoc with 150 plus sites, Norsk Gjenvinning planning 20 machines by 2028, and a strategic partner MOU covering 90 quarry and aggregate sites. [pipeline timeline 1 Sep 2026; all-hands 3 Sep 2026] The infrastructure being built now decides what those sites can get later. Requirements have to be written down with real values before the architecture hardens.

Chapter 2 · Domain primer

The quarry, from the top.

Rock is blasted from the face, crushed, screened into sizes, stockpiled, and sold by the tonne over a weighbridge. The wheel loader touches almost every step.

The material flow

  1. Blast. The face is drilled and blasted on a schedule. Everything stops and everyone leaves the zone while it happens.
  2. Muck out. Blasted rock is loaded by excavator or wheel loader into haulers, or moved straight to the primary crusher. Norwegian sites call the heap "salve" or "røys".
  3. Crush and screen. Primary, then secondary and tertiary crushers reduce the rock. Screens sort it into fractions. Conveyors drop each fraction onto its own stockpile.
  4. Stockpile management. The loader keeps the piles clear of the conveyor drop, blends fractions when a product needs it, moves material between piles.
  5. Load out. Customer trucks arrive, the weighbridge opens a ticket, the loader fills the truck, the truck weighs out. This is where the quarry gets paid.

The loader has three jobs in this chain: feed the crusher, keep the piles right, load the trucks. Which one dominates depends on the site, and that decides what "good" means for the machine.

Numbers to hold on to

A bucket on an L180-class loader is around 6 tonnes. A site on continuous load-out can move around 600 tonnes an hour with one machine. [Ronny, 11 Sep 2026, illustrative] A four-axle Norwegian truck takes 17 to 18 tonnes, three buckets. [Feiring deal page] Where the loader feeds a mobile crusher, the crusher sets the pace: about 650 t/h on a single 0/150 product, 500 on 0/32, and 300 plus 150 plus 150 when three fractions run at once. [NCC site walk, 21 Aug 2026, crusher capacity, not a requirement on Hive]

Labour is a small share of the cost in this world. At NCC's mobile crushing setup it is 2 to 3 percent, diesel is 20 to 25 percent, and the rest is the machines. [NCC site walk, 21 Aug 2026] That is why the metrics chapter asks engineering to log fuel per tonne from day one, not only tonnes and uptime.

What "customer happy" means here. The crusher never waits for material. The piles do not bury the conveyor. Trucks leave inside the weighbridge window. Nobody walks into the machine's area unannounced.

Asphalt plant. Cold feed bins on a clock.
Eurovia at Thurrock, UK. Peab's Swedish eco-site as the reference pattern.

An asphalt plant draws aggregate fractions from cold feed bins in a fixed recipe. The loader keeps each bin topped up from its bay. Miss a bin and the mix goes off spec or the plant stops. Distances are short, the pattern repeats, and the pace is set by the plant.

Eurovia, Thurrock. Six feeders and ten bays in pilot scope, a second area with ten feeders and sixteen bays out of scope. The customer's own KPI framework references plant demand around 100 plus t/h and a diesel loader burning 160 to 180 litres over a 6 to 7 hour shift. Industrial plant. Fixed surroundings, high stakes.
Yara at Herøya, our first larger customer. 18 months under contract, autonomy in production from October 2026.

A Volvo L120H moving fertilizer inside one of Europe's largest ammonia facilities. Pile to hopper, fixed surroundings. Started on tele-remote; the site has asked for more autonomy in the cycle since. Safety is a light barrier around the area: the machine stops if the beam is broken. [use-case deck; Yara deal page]

The shift, as Yara specified it. Eight hours. Drive from parking to the bin area through mixed traffic once, in the morning. Then 200 cycles: drive to bin, fill about 4 tonnes, drive to the unloading area, wait until it is ready, unload left, middle or right. Each step about half a minute. Maximum emptying rate 1.6 tonnes a minute, so the loader waits on the plant, not the other way round. Bucket change once a day, or up to ten times an hour when two products alternate. Three evenings a month a special product: 65 cycles in mixed traffic, press a button and wait for the silo. Waste runs seven times a day. Fuel once a day, ten minutes. [Yara Appendix 1, Requirements Rev.2]

Where it stands. Load-and-carry cycle averages 2 min 30 s. Hive was at 8 percent of the volume when the operation paused in August 2025. Yara's gate for the restart: one week of stable remote operation with no loss of volume. [Yara deal page, 12 Aug 2026]

Mine. The orchestration layer arrives.
Mariana Minerals in Utah is building the system that will send us tasks. Brønnøy Kalk shows what a fully autonomous site already looks like.

Mariana Minerals, Copper One, Utah. Two CAT 992 loaders in the 100 tonne class, three times the Feiring machine, running 24/7. A dozer and an excavator on 12/7. And it is the product we want to sell everyone else eventually: an orchestration layer that controls our machines and, later, other vendors' machines on the same site.

Brønnøy Kalk, Velfjord. About 2 Mt a year of calcite marble. Nine autonomous Volvo FH trucks on a 5 km route, safety driver removed in August 2023, over 1 Mt and 220,000 km driven. One Volvo L350 is the only manned machine inside the autonomous zone. [research note 15 Jun 2026] That loader is the shape of the opportunity.

Two phases. In the proof period Hive supplies the machine, our own Volvo L180, run remote, so the customer does not tie up one of theirs. When the operating contract starts we retrofit one of the customer's own machines, two to three days, non-destructive. The customer owns the machine, Hive supplies the operators. What the platform is, in five layers

Safety System at the bottom, then Machine Control, Operator Platform, Autonomy, and Physical AI on top, with Connectivity and Data Logging as rails along the side. Each layer depends on the one beneath it; nothing runs unless the safety layer is present and healthy. Teleoperation is the first three layers sold on their own. Autonomy adds the fourth. Physical AI is all five. A closed CAN bus, as on Caterpillar, is a layer-two obstacle and blocks everything above it. [Hive Platform definition, 5 Aug 2026]

What still costs cycle time under remote operation.
Five gaps found at Hadeland in summer 2026. None of them stop the machine loading. They stop it loading fast.
  1. Brake pedal fidelity. Three pedals, two brake. One is so hard the operator avoids it, the other behaves differently from one attempt to the next. Kenneth compensates by driving slowly throughout. Roar's hypothesis: the torque converter cuts on brake instead of keeping drive, which no experienced operator accepts. Cycle times taken with unstable brake behaviour measure the defect, not the capability.
  2. Depth perception. The operator cannot judge lateral distance to the truck from camera feeds, so loads end up off-centre. The operator brakes to a full stop before lifting, takes the bump, loses a couple of hundred kilos, drives over the spill next pass.
  3. Fixed geometry beats vision. Front axle to truck is the same distance on every standard L180, and nearly all trucks are about 2.5 m wide. A fixed distance reference to the truck side solves centring without solving depth perception. An open ML problem becomes a bounded sensor-and-UI problem.
  4. No tilt indication in the operator view. Matters for centring and for tipping safety. Volvo A30 haulers already carry the gyro.
  5. Uncommanded e-stops break flow, and the log lies. Every stop restarts the cycle. The log reported one e-stop as a station button press that never happened, so root-causing cannot rely on it until fixed. Network drops under the rock face on the upper bench look the same in the field.

[remote truck loading application gaps, wiki, 5 Aug 2026; Feiring deal page]

The autonomy path, honestly.
Rule-based autonomy runs zones at Yara. The learning-based policy (VLA) works blasted rock on video and is not production-ready.

Engineering's own words on 2 September, about the VLA: "very far from anything production-ready. Fine for a demo video, I would not trust it in production." Time to production-ready: "maybe a month, probably not, maybe three, maybe six, maybe a year." The VLA will hurt throughput before it helps. That is why no customer KPI is set on VLA cycle time. [Mariana KPI prep, internal meeting 2 Sep 2026]

The co-pilot path: teleoperate first, the system learns from every intervention, the autonomy ratio rises, one operator watches more machines. Release 2.3 (new cabinet, harness, camera positions, GMSL cameras) is the hardware baseline for scaling and is slipping. [HM platform roadmap and hardware stability, 28 Aug 2026]

Hours, not just seconds.
Availability is where the money is. A fast cycle on a machine that stands still is worth nothing.

A cab operator works one shift with breaks, and the machine stands still when they do. A remote operated machine can be handed between operators and in principle runs the plant's hours, not the operator's. That is the productivity argument. It only holds if the machine is actually available, which is why availability is the first metric in chapter 8 and the one with contractual thresholds.

Duty cycle decides the business case. At Mariana the loaders run 8,736 hours a year and availability pricing wins. A readymix loader at CEMEX runs 1 to 3 hours a day and the same pricing loses against a part-time operator. Below about 0.42 operator FTE per shift, a license priced on availability costs more than the wage it replaces. [availability pricing breaks on low utilisation, wiki, 12 Aug 2026; CEMEX account brief]

For calibration: fully autonomous mining fleets report availability in the high eighties to mid nineties, and OEE studies on mobile mining equipment often land far below the manufacturing "world class" 85 percent. Those are dedicated haul trucks on closed roads. Use them as a frame for what to measure, not as a target. [research note 11 Sep 2026]

Chapter 11

Glossary.

Muckpile
The heap of blasted rock at the face before it is moved. Norwegian: røys, salve.
Stockpile
A heap of one product fraction, usually under a conveyor drop. Norwegian: haug, lager.
Fraction
A size class of crushed rock, written as smallest/largest in mm: 0/32, 8/16, 0/150.
Crusher feed
Keeping the primary crusher's hopper supplied. The task that never calls and never waits.
Load-out, HGV loading
Filling a customer truck from a stockpile against a weighbridge ticket. Norwegian: utlasting. UK: HGV, heavy goods vehicle.
Load and carry
A cycle with a long travel leg, as at Yara: the loader carries the bucket to a hopper some distance away.
Weighbridge ticket
The record of product, weight in, weight out. The only written trace of a task on most sites. Norwegian: vektseddel.
Cold feed bins
The hoppers at an asphalt plant that hold each aggregate fraction for the mix.
Hold-up
NCC's term: production time lost because the Hive-operated machine could not keep up.
Cycle time
Seconds for one bucket: spot, fill, lift, travel, dump, return.
Bucket fill factor
Payload divided by rated bucket capacity.
Pass
One bucket into a truck. Passes per truck is Mariana's "pass match".
Glass-to-glass
Camera to operator screen latency.
Motion-to-motion
Joystick to machine response latency.
Geofence
The boundary the machine will not cross, and inside which people are not expected.
Remote operator
Hive staff in Kristiansand driving or supervising the machine.
Task coordinator
Hive staff who takes the site's call and turns it into work for the machine.
HOS
Hive Operating Station, the operations centre and the physical station. Internal term only. Externally: "operations centre in Kristiansand".
Supervised autonomy
The machine runs the cycle, a person watches and can take over. Our product category. Not "remote control".
VLA
Vision-language-action policy, the learning-based autonomy layer. Works blasted rock on video, not production-ready.
Retrofit
Fitting our kit to the customer's own machine. Two to three days, non-destructive.
OptiShift, CDC, RBB
The three Volvo options that make a loader integrable: OptiShift transmission with the RBB brake valve, Comfort Drive Control steering, and electric lift and tilt.
ODD
Operational design domain. The conditions the system is built to work in. Chapter 7.
PL d, PL e
Performance levels for safety functions under ISO 13849. E-stops and safe state at PL e; person detection at PL d when supervision is reduced.
