Act II · The forcing function
03 / 14
The forcing function

Manual labour is constrained.

Automation is no longer optional because the alternative — hiring more operators — is not viable. Three constraints, all structural, all getting worse.

Supply
30–50%

Annual operator turnover in EU logistics. Workforce shrinking, aging out faster than replaced. Every rotation re-opens a productivity gap.

Cost
~70%

Of a 2-shift forklift's loaded TCO is the operator. Wages up 4–7% per year. Shift premiums, sickness cover and onboarding outpace machine cost.

Reliability
5×

Forklift-operator injury rate vs. average warehouse role. Shifts go uncovered. Safety incidents block throughput. Iron sits idle 34% of the time.

The labour you can hire isn't enough. The labour you can't hire is the bottleneck.
Act III · The honest problem
04 / 14
The honest problem

AMRs were supposed to replace labour. They can't. Yet.

Every operator who ran a pilot has met the same wall. The gap between what AMRs can do today and what they would need to do to viably replace human labour is large — and most operators blamed the robot.

What AMRs do today

Conveyor belts on wheels.

  • Fixed routes, pre-mapped environments
  • One task, one machine, one workflow
  • Structured floors only — no edge cases
  • Stops on exception, waits for a human
  • Per-task integration, per-vendor stack
Useful in narrow lanes. Not a labour replacement.
THE GAP
What replacing labour requires

What humans actually do.

  • Mixed tasks across the day, no script
  • Floor and yard, indoor and outdoor
  • Dynamic, messy, unstructured environments
  • Judgment under exception, no human nearby
  • Cross-equipment, cross-site generalization
The job an operator does on day one.
Every operator who ran a pilot has met this gap. Most blamed the robot.
